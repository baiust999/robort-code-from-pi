# 2. Related Work

This section places the system within the search-and-rescue (SAR) robotics literature along the four challenges of Section 1.2: a bounded stop time (C1), freedom from single points of failure (C2), two-way victim interaction under constrained resources (C3) and affordability (C4). Section 2.1 positions the work against prior reviews and defines the levels of operator involvement used throughout the paper. Section 2.2 reviews field deployments, benchmarks and recent platforms. Sections 2.3 to 2.5 review fail-safe and fault-tolerant architectures, operator- and victim-side interaction, and real-time media on edge hardware. Section 2.6 states the gap and compares representative platforms in Table 2.1.

Sources were identified by keyword searches of Crossref and OpenAlex (terms combining search and rescue, rescue robot, teleoperation, victim, low-cost, fail-safe, WebRTC and edge computing; last searched October 2026) and by backward citation from the reviews of Section 2.1. ## 2.1 Prior Reviews and Levels of Operator Involvement

Reviews of SAR robotics organise the field along different axes. Murphy formulated a domain theory of robot-assisted search, with workflow and information-flow models, from eight years of HRI experience in urban search and rescue [@murphy2004hri]. Liu and Nejat surveyed robotic urban search and rescue from the control perspective [@liu2013]. Queralta et al. reviewed multi-robot SAR systems with a focus on the algorithms for multi-robot coordination and perception [@queralta2020]. Delmerico et al. surveyed ground, aerial, marine and amphibious systems and human-robot control interfaces against the needs of first responders, drawing on expert opinions from emergency-response stakeholders, and observe that field-deployable rescue technology has requirements for robustness, speed, versatility and ease of use that the state of the art in robotics research may not meet [@delmerico2019]. Schneider and Wildermuth reviewed SAR robotics as an applied benchmark for field robotics and set out SAR-specific requirements on each component of a robotic system [@schneider2016]. Kareem et al. analysed 74 papers published between 2016 and 2024, classified the platforms as aerial, ground or marine, examined their sensors, autonomous navigation and human-robot interaction (HRI) capabilities, and proposed a five-layer architecture of HRI, Perception, Navigation, Control and Robot layers; they identify multi-robot coordination, AI-based real-time decision-making and energy efficiency as open challenges [@kareem2026]. Sinha et al. synthesised more than 190 studies from 2020 to 2025 restricted to unmanned ground vehicles (UGVs) for disaster reconnaissance, and identify energy limitations, robustness in harsh conditions and the lack of standardised benchmarks as remaining problems [@sinha2026]. Bakzadeh et al. reviewed the coal-mine rescue robots documented over three decades against design and functionality requirements, noting that comprehensive guidelines for robot design in harsh underground environments have not been established and that two-way voice is a fundamental functionality of most of the mine rescue robots they review [@bakzadeh2026]. Tong et al. reviewed robotised SAR with an emphasis on deficiencies in perception systems [@tong2024], and Chitikena et al. discuss the ethical and design issues raised by robotic and cognitive technology in the response phase of SAR, with emphasis on snake robots for confined spaces [@chitikena2023]. These reviews organise the field by HRI workflow, control approach, coordination, readiness for deployment, system architecture, platform class or design requirement; neither the timing of the stop path nor the cost of victim interaction on the robot is the axis along which they organise it.

Levels of robot autonomy range from teleoperation to fully autonomous systems [@beer2014], and Schneider and Wildermuth observe that SAR robots, although they could in principle sit anywhere on that spectrum, are in practice either tele-operated or semi-autonomous [@schneider2016]. Along this spectrum, this paper distinguishes four levels of operator involvement: Teleoperation, in which the operator drives the robot directly; Supervisory Control, in which the operator sets goals and the robot executes them; Shared-Collaborative Control, in which operator and robot share decision authority; and Full Autonomy, in which no human is in the control loop during the mission. The system presented here is a Teleoperation system. The choice follows the deployment evidence rather than the research trend: Daun and von Stryk, drawing on reports of past deployments and workshops with first responders, state that rescue robots are typically teleoperated in actual disasters [@daun2023], and Chen et al. conclude that teleoperation will remain an important default mode even for autonomous and semi-autonomous systems [@chen2007]. Daun and von Stryk also identify a need for approaches that combine the complementary capabilities of human operators and robotic assistance functions [@daun2023]. Higher levels of involvement are treated in Section 8 as extensions that must remain subordinate to the stop guarantee of Section 3.5.

## 2.2 Field Deployments, Benchmarks and Recent Platforms

**Field deployments.** The case for a human in the control loop, and for treating the radio link as unreliable, rests on field evidence. The response to the World Trade Center collapse in September 2001 is the first known use of robots for urban search and rescue; Murphy summarises the robots' performance there in terms of mobility, sensors, control, communications and power [@murphy2004]. Casper and Murphy's post-hoc analysis of the same response, the first study of HRI during an unstaged rescue, produced 17 findings and 11 recommendations, including calls for more research into perceptual and assistive interfaces and for formal models of the robot's state [@casper2003]. At the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear power plants in 2011, tests in a comparable plant indicated that robust wireless communication would not be possible inside the reactor buildings, so the robot deployed there was fitted with a wired communication device [@nagatani2013]; Kawatsuma et al. identify organisation, operation scheme and systemisation as the major lessons of that response [@kawatsuma2012]. After the August 2016 earthquake in Amatrice, Italy, ground and aerial robots were deployed to collect data for 3D models of two badly damaged churches [@kruijff2016]. At Fukushima the deployment had to be designed around the radio link failing; this paper therefore treats loss or degradation of the link to the operator as an expected operating condition rather than a rare fault, which motivates C1 and C2.

**Benchmarks.** Standard test methods for response robots have been developed and refined through competitions [@jacoff2012], and the DARPA Subterranean Challenge (2018-2021) evaluated integrated systems for complex underground environments in physical and simulated contests [@chung2023]. These benchmarks evaluate the capabilities of integrated platforms. The indoor measurements of Section 5 do not use the standardised test apparatus, and results are therefore not directly comparable with benchmark scores.

**Recent platforms.** Recent platforms concentrate their contribution on mechanical capability, perception or communication range. Among teleoperated designs, Paripreksya 2.0 is a tracked robot with four assistive flippers and a 4-DoF manipulator, built on ROS and evaluated through scores at the World Robot Summit 2020, where it received the best-in-class dexterity award [@megalingam2022]. TXRob is a low-cost tracked explorer with a camera, gas sensors and a real-time data display, which a human operator monitors and controls remotely; it was tested on uneven surfaces in a simulated disaster environment [@verano2022]. Pramono et al. built a 3D-printed robot from off-the-shelf parts with remote-controlled movement, real-time video, geopositioning and remote lighting, derived its requirements from a literature review and interviews with first responders, and report Bluetooth communication up to 10 m in laboratory tests and video transmission up to 12 m in field experiments [@pramono2024]. Khattab et al. present SERR, a robot controlled from a smartphone application, which fuses visual, thermal and audio data in a CNN-LSTM survivor detector validated by MATLAB simulation on USGS-PAGER data [@khattab2025]. Manuel et al. propose a LoRa LPWAN communication architecture for sites where communication infrastructure has been destroyed, with a robot, Rescuer, monitored and controlled from a remote base station in Gazebo simulation and a laboratory test setup [@manuel2022]. Cafolla et al. built a universal add-on controller that connects to any robot accepting text commands and adds voice-guided control, 3D reconstruction and person tracking, tested on a drone and a quadruped in wind of 23 km/h and noise up to 100 dB [@cafolla2026].

Other recent platforms contribute mobility or detection. Jia et al. designed a ground-air amphibious UAV whose separation-cage structure addresses the sensor occlusion of earlier caged drones, with a 1.2 kg prototype reaching 40 min endurance at full 1 kg payload [@jia2025]. Liu et al. present the mechanical structure and a locomotion control framework for a wheeled hexapod rescue device [@liu2026hexapod]. Yamauchi et al. developed an active scope camera, a continuum robot for narrow spaces carrying 80 distributed sensors including inertial measurement units, microphones, speakers and vibration sensors, and demonstrated a survivor search in simulated rubble at the Fukushima Robot Test Field [@yamauchi2022]. Kim et al. propose a low-cost sensing and control architecture for a SAR assistant robot that locates survivors with a thermal infrared sensor array and approaches them along a generated trajectory [@kim2023]. Raghavan et al. fine-tuned a YOLOv8 model to detect victims under rubble (93.1% mean average precision) and deployed it on a Jetson Nano carried by a mobile robotic vehicle with cloud-based alerting [@raghavan2026], and Cantizani-Estepa et al. showed that Bluetooth Low Energy wearables can be detected by terrestrial and aerial robots up to 15 m away at transmission power typical of a smartphone [@cantizani2022].

Affordability is claimed more often than it is quantified. TXRob is described as low-cost [@verano2022], Pramono et al. describe their robot as affordable [@pramono2024], Kim et al. state that their prototype can be built from inexpensive, commonly available parts [@kim2023], Raghavan et al. describe the Jetson Nano as a low-cost processor [@raghavan2026], and Sinha et al. list microcontroller-based control, LoRa communication and DC motors among the building blocks of current reconnaissance UGVs [@sinha2026]; yet none of the platforms in Table 2.1 states a numeric build cost. Section 5.9 therefore compares cost qualitatively and reports this robot's own figure as an estimate (C4).

## 2.3 Fail-Safe and Fault-Tolerant Architectures for Low-Cost Mobile Robots

**Bounded stop.** Stopping a machine when its command stream fails is established practice. IEC 60204-1 defines stop categories for the electrical equipment of machines, of which category 0 is stopping by immediate removal of power to the machine actuators, an uncontrolled stop [@iec60204]. The dead-man principle is not new; the question for a low-cost robot is where the stop decision runs and whether its timing can be bounded. A stop decision taken by a Linux process inherits the scheduling, memory and crash behaviour of that process. Pont presents design patterns for time-triggered embedded systems, in which tasks are released by a periodic timer rather than by asynchronous events, so that their timing can be predicted [@pont2001]. The firmware of Section 3.5 uses a co-operative, time-based scheduler in this style, although two blocking sensor reads add up to about 50 ms of loop jitter (Section 3.5.2): a 10 ms safety task on the microcontroller sets the pulse-width modulation (PWM) outputs to zero and pulls the shared driver-enable line low when no valid command has arrived within the dead-man window. This stop behaves like a category-0 stop, with immediate removal of drive and no ramp, and does not depend on the Linux processes above it (C1); it is not a safety-rated implementation in the sense of the standard. Network delay raises a related but different problem: Narasimhan et al. construct a run-time monitor that restricts a networked robot to actions that remain safe under delayed sensory observations, given the expected and maximum network latency, and demonstrate it on an F1/10th vehicle over congested Wi-Fi [@narasimhan2024]. Such a monitor reasons about late information; the dead-man timer handles the case in which commands stop arriving altogether, and the two are complementary.

**Process supervision.** Above the firmware, the risk is a single software process whose failure takes down both control and media. Candea et al. argue that building systems to recover fast may be more productive than aiming for systems that never fail, and that because recovery can itself fail, failures should be managed with multiple lines of defence [@candea2004]. Schrick et al. propose a robot control architecture built from microservices and a hierarchical finite-state machine that reduces centralised logic and so increases concurrency, interruptibility and fail-safety, and demonstrate it on a delivery robot operated in public space [@schrick2025]. Ramesh et al. introduce "robot vitals", lower-level metrics combined into a single "robot health" estimate, to detect performance degradation of remote mobile robots at run time so that a human supervisor can intervene [@ramesh2022]. Koczi and Sarosi, in a scoping review of 121 studies on safety engineering for humanoid robots, find that most work concentrates on physical safety, including fault-tolerant control, while cybersecurity, standardisation and socio-ethical aspects are addressed less frequently [@koczi2025]. None of these works targets a single-board rescue robot. The three-process design of Section 3.7 applies the same principles on a Raspberry Pi: a supervisor restarts the control and media processes independently (fast recovery), the firmware stop does not depend on any of them (a second line of defence), and the mission state of Section 3.7.3 exposes degradation to the operator (C2). Among the rescue platforms of Table 2.1, none reports a hardware-level stop guarantee or supervised process recovery.

## 2.4 Human-Robot Interaction: Operator and Victim

**Operator side.** Situation awareness, which Endsley models through its role in dynamic human decision making [@endsley1995], is the recurring operator-side concern. Chen et al. reviewed more than 150 papers on human performance in teleoperation and summarise the performance decrements caused by limited video bandwidth, time lags, low frame rates, lack of proprioception and two-dimensional views [@chen2007]. Luz et al., working on teleoperation for planetary exploration, note that even small amounts of latency can compromise robot safety, cause goal overshoot and raise cognitive workload, and evaluate augmented interfaces in a virtual environment for a robot operated under 3 s of latency [@luz2023]. For mine rescue, Bakzadeh et al. map cognitive load, situational awareness, trust and attentional control to interface design principles and recommend layered feedback, multimodal communication and adaptive interfaces [@bakzadeh2025]; in interviews with ten mine-rescue personnel and subject-matter experts, they further identify a need for real-time situational data and customisable interfaces, with communication capabilities and system reliability among the recurring themes [@bakzadeh2026b]. Ruan et al. propose semantic indicators, including signs of human activity, that summarise how information-rich a scene is and so when it requires the expert operator's attention, tested on a Jackal robot in a mock-up disaster environment [@ruan2025]. The dashboard of this system (Section 3.6) serves the same goal through telemetry with explicit staleness timeouts and a mission state (Section 3.7.3).

**Robot to rescue team.** Akgun et al. studied affect as an additional communication channel for SAR robots in three online studies with 112, 223 and 151 participants; in the third, LED-based affective expressions on an appearance-constrained field robot improved the situational awareness of participants acting as rescue workers in simulated SAR situations [@akgun2023].

**Victim side.** Communication with the trapped person is a recognised requirement: Schneider and Wildermuth state that a SAR robot should be able to provide audio-visual communication between the operator and trapped persons that allows basic diagnosis by an emergency responder [@schneider2016]. The time involved is long. Srinivasan et al. report that, on average, four to ten hours pass between the discovery of a victim and extrication, during which a rescue robot remains with the victim to monitor their condition, and argue that the robot must interact in a socially appropriate way to keep the victim calm and engaged [@srinivasan2012]. The most developed line of work on this role is the Survivor Buddy project. Bethel and Murphy applied non-facial and non-verbal affective expression to robot-assisted victim assessment, in which a robot operates within about three metres of a victim [@bethel2007]. Survivor Buddy itself is a four-degree-of-freedom robot head whose face is a multimedia touch-screen monitor, used to study robots as social media that act as intermediaries between trapped survivors and the outside world, including doctors, rescuers and family members [@murphy2010]; a second version was 50% less expensive, 78% lighter and up to 700% faster than the first [@murphy2011]. That work concentrates on affect, trust and socially appropriate behaviour and was evaluated in an informal demonstration for a public-television science programme and in simulated urban search and rescue scenarios [@murphy2010; @srinivasan2012].

Two-way voice is also not new on fielded rescue robots: Bakzadeh et al. report it as a fundamental functionality of most of the mine rescue robots they review, for example the ANDROS Wolverine V2 and the CUMT-V [@bakzadeh2026]. These are specialised mine-rescue platforms, outside the low-cost scope of Table 2.1. In the recent platforms reviewed in Section 2.2, by contrast, the trapped person is mainly an object of detection. Victims are detected by vision, thermal sensing, radio beacons or multimodal fusion [@raghavan2026; @khattab2025; @kim2023; @cantizani2022], or the platform reports no victim-facing channel at all [@megalingam2022; @verano2022; @pramono2024; @cafolla2026; @jia2025]. The active scope camera of Yamauchi et al. carries microphones and speakers among its distributed sensors, which the authors describe as information-gathering functions for survivor search [@yamauchi2022] [VERIFY: full text, whether the speakers are used to talk with victims]. Among the platforms in Table 2.1, the one that provisions a speaker and microphone for communication with victims reports that this capability is pending implementation in its prototype [@khattab2025]. Two-way victim communication is therefore an established requirement, a common feature of fielded mine-rescue robots and the subject of a substantial HRI literature; what no recent low-cost platform in Table 2.1 demonstrates is two-way audio and video with the victim on single-board hardware, and none reports what it costs in CPU, power and bandwidth on the robot (C3).

## 2.5 Real-Time Media and Computing on Edge Hardware

WebRTC has been used for robot teleoperation with strict latency targets. Shi et al. built a remote dental surgery robot on an optimised Amazon Kinesis Video Streams WebRTC framework with two video and two data channels and report end-to-end latency below 50 ms, 34.2% lower than their baseline configuration [@shi2025webrtc]. Tiberkak et al. built a WebRTC web platform through which several operators control a mobile manipulator simultaneously, with a priority system for parallel tasks and a chat channel between operators, validated on an emulator of the robot over the Internet [@tiberkak2023]; Sections 3.8 and 3.9.4 address the same multi-operator problem with explicit arbitration and floor control. Both were deployed over the Internet or through a cloud service; the robot described here runs both WebRTC endpoints and signalling on the local network with no Internet dependence (Sections 3.2 and 3.9). For audio, the Opus codec is designed for interactive speech and audio and supports frame sizes from 2.5 ms to 60 ms [@valin2012], which lets a design trade packet rate against delay; Section 3.9.5 uses 60 ms packets instead of the 20 ms frames that the specification describes as a good choice for most applications, to cut per-packet CPU work on the Raspberry Pi. Comparing such systems requires a defined latency metric: Bachhuber and Steinbach present a glass-to-glass delay measurement system with 0.5 ms precision at a 2 kHz sampling rate [@bachhuber2016]; Section 5.2 defines the same glass-to-glass metric, to be measured with a simpler and coarser clock-photograph procedure; that measurement is pending.

Where the computing runs is itself a design choice. Militano et al. describe the cloud-to-edge-to-IoT continuum as a way to meet the low-delay requirements of SAR operations, while noting that building such a continuum is still an open challenge [@militano2023]. Raghavan et al. run detection onboard on a Jetson Nano but send alerts through the cloud [@raghavan2026], and Cafolla et al. use cloud-based connectivity for streaming and data flow [@cafolla2026]; both presume network infrastructure that a collapsed site may not provide, a concern that also motivates the infrastructure-free LoRa architecture of Manuel et al. [@manuel2022] and the wired link used at Fukushima [@nagatani2013]. Running control, two-way media and supervision together on a single-board computer without the cloud is not characterised for a rescue robot in these works; Section 5.5 reports the resulting resource budget.

The gap that Table 2.1 makes visible is a combination rather than a single missing feature, and none of its parts is claimed as a first. Teleoperation is the mode used in real deployments [@daun2023] and is expected to remain a default mode [@chen2007], and field experience shows that the link to the operator degrades or fails [@nagatani2013]. Stop categories for machinery are standardised [@iec60204] and fast recovery by restart is an established dependability strategy [@candea2004], yet no recent rescue platform in Table 2.1 reports a stop time bounded independently of its higher-level software (C1) or supervised recovery of its software processes (C2). Two-way communication with the trapped person is a stated requirement [@schneider2016], a common feature of fielded mine-rescue robots [@bakzadeh2026] and the subject of a substantial HRI literature [@bethel2007; @murphy2010; @srinivasan2012], yet among recent low-cost platforms the victim appears mainly as a detection target, the one planned voice channel is not yet implemented [@khattab2025], and none reports two-way audio and video with the victim or its resource cost on the robot (C3). Low cost is asserted without a figure (C4). Section 3 describes a system that brings these established mechanisms together on commodity hardware, and Section 5 reports which of these properties the current measurements support and which remain pending.
