# 2. Related Work

This section places the system within the search-and-rescue (SAR) robotics literature along the four challenges of Section 1.2: a bounded stop time (C1), freedom from single points of failure (C2), two-way victim interaction under constrained resources (C3) and affordability (C4). Section 2.1 positions the work against prior reviews and defines the levels of operator involvement used throughout the paper. Sections 2.2 to 2.5 review rescue robot platforms, fail-safe architectures, operator- and victim-side interaction, and real-time media on edge hardware. Section 2.6 states the gap and compares representative platforms in Table 2.1. Every statement about an individual paper is taken from that paper.

## 2.1 Prior Reviews and Levels of Operator Involvement

Reviews of SAR robotics organise the field along different axes. Liu and Nejat surveyed robotic urban search and rescue from the control perspective [@liu2013]. Kareem et al. analysed 74 papers published between 2016 and 2024, classified the platforms as aerial, ground or marine, examined their sensors, autonomous navigation and human-robot interaction (HRI) capabilities, and proposed a five-layer architecture of HRI, Perception, Navigation, Control and Robot layers; they identify multi-robot coordination, AI-based real-time decision-making and energy efficiency as open challenges [@kareem2026]. Sinha et al. synthesised more than 190 studies from 2020 to 2025 restricted to unmanned ground vehicles (UGVs) for disaster reconnaissance, and identify energy limitations, robustness in harsh conditions and the lack of standardised benchmarks as remaining problems [@sinha2026]. Bakzadeh et al. reviewed the coal-mine rescue robots documented over three decades against design and functionality requirements, noting that comprehensive guidelines for robot design in harsh underground environments have not been established [@bakzadeh2026]. Tong et al. reviewed robotised SAR with an emphasis on deficiencies in perception systems [@tong2024]. These reviews organise the field by control approach, system architecture, platform class or design requirement; the safety of the control path and the interaction between robot and trapped person are not their organising concerns.

This paper uses four levels of operator involvement: Teleoperation, in which the operator drives the robot directly; Supervisory Control, in which the operator sets goals and the robot executes them; Shared-Collaborative Control, in which operator and robot share decision authority; and Full Autonomy, in which no human is in the control loop during the mission. The system presented here is a Teleoperation system. The choice follows the deployment evidence rather than the research trend: Daun and von Stryk, drawing on reports of past deployments and workshops with first responders, state that rescue robots are typically teleoperated in actual disasters [@daun2023], and Chen et al. conclude that teleoperation will remain an important default mode even for autonomous and semi-autonomous systems [@chen2007]. Higher levels of involvement are treated in Section 8 as extensions that must remain subordinate to the stop guarantee of Section 3.5.

## 2.2 Rescue Robot Platforms and Their Cost

The case for a human in the control loop rests on field evidence. Casper and Murphy's post-hoc analysis of robot use at the World Trade Center, the first study of HRI during an unstaged rescue, produced 17 findings and 11 recommendations, including calls for more research into perceptual and assistive interfaces and for formal models of the robot's state [@casper2003]. Daun and von Stryk identify a need for approaches that combine the complementary capabilities of human operators and robotic assistance functions [@daun2023].

Recent platforms concentrate their contribution on mechanical capability or perception. Among teleoperated designs, Paripreksya 2.0 is a tracked robot with four assistive flippers and a 4-DoF manipulator, built on ROS and evaluated through scores at the World Robot Summit 2020, where it received the best-in-class dexterity award [@megalingam2022]. TXRob is a low-cost tracked explorer with a camera, gas sensors and a real-time data display, which a human operator monitors and controls remotely; it was tested on uneven surfaces in a simulated disaster environment [@verano2022]. Khattab et al. present SERR, a robot controlled from a smartphone application, which fuses visual, thermal and audio data in a CNN-LSTM survivor detector validated by MATLAB simulation on USGS-PAGER data [@khattab2025]. Cafolla et al. built a universal add-on controller that connects to any robot accepting text commands and adds voice-guided control, 3D reconstruction and person tracking, tested on a drone and a quadruped in wind of 23 km/h and noise up to 100 dB [@cafolla2026].

Other recent platforms contribute mobility or detection. Jia et al. designed a ground-air amphibious UAV whose separation-cage structure removes the sensor occlusion of earlier caged drones, with a 1.2 kg prototype reaching 40 min endurance at full 1 kg payload [@jia2025]. Liu et al. present the mechanical structure and a locomotion control framework for a wheeled hexapod rescue device [@liu2026hexapod]. Raghavan et al. fine-tuned a YOLOv8 model to detect victims under rubble (93.1% mean average precision) and deployed it on a Jetson Nano carried by a mobile robotic vehicle with cloud-based alerting [@raghavan2026]. None of these papers reports, in its abstract, a bounded stop time or supervision of its software processes as a contribution (Table 2.1).

Affordability is claimed more often than it is quantified. TXRob is described as low-cost [@verano2022], Raghavan et al. describe the Jetson Nano as a low-cost processor [@raghavan2026], and Sinha et al. list microcontroller-based control, LoRa communication and DC motors among the building blocks of current reconnaissance UGVs [@sinha2026]; yet none of the platforms in Table 2.1 states a numeric build cost. Section 5.9 therefore compares cost qualitatively and reports this robot's own figure as an estimate (C4).

## 2.3 Fail-Safe and Fault-Tolerant Architectures for Low-Cost Mobile Robots

A bounded stop time requires that the stop decision does not depend on a non-real-time operating system. Pont presents design patterns for time-triggered embedded systems, in which tasks are released by a periodic timer rather than by asynchronous events, so that their timing can be predicted [@pont2001]. The firmware of Section 3.5 follows this pattern: a 10 ms safety task on the microcontroller removes motor drive when no valid command has arrived within the dead-man window, independently of the Linux processes above it (C1).

Above the firmware, the risk is a single software process whose failure takes down both control and media. Schrick et al. propose a robot control architecture built from microservices and a hierarchical finite-state machine that reduces centralised logic and so increases concurrency, interruptibility and fail-safety, and demonstrate it on a delivery robot operated in public space [@schrick2025]. Koczi and Sarosi, in a scoping review of 121 studies on safety engineering for humanoid robots, find that most work concentrates on physical safety, including fault-tolerant control, while software robustness, secure real-time communication and standardisation receive less attention [@koczi2025]. Both works concern platforms and budgets well above a single-board rescue robot. The three-process design of Section 3.7, in which a supervisor restarts the control and media processes independently, applies the same isolation principle on a Raspberry Pi (C2). Among the rescue platforms of Table 2.1, none reports a hardware-level stop guarantee or supervised process recovery.

## 2.4 Human-Robot Interaction: Operator and Victim

**Operator side.** Chen et al. reviewed more than 150 papers on human performance in teleoperation and summarise the performance decrements caused by limited video bandwidth, time lags, low frame rates, lack of proprioception and two-dimensional views [@chen2007]. For mine rescue, Bakzadeh et al. map cognitive load, situational awareness, trust and attentional control to interface design principles and recommend layered feedback, multimodal communication and adaptive interfaces [@bakzadeh2025]. Ruan et al. propose semantic indicators, including signs of human activity, that summarise how information-rich a scene is and so when it requires the expert operator's attention, tested on a Jackal robot in a mock-up disaster environment [@ruan2025]. These works treat the operator as the human the interface serves; the dashboard of this system (Section 3.6) follows the same goal through telemetry with explicit staleness timeouts and a mission state (Section 3.7.3).

**Robot to rescue team.** Akgun et al. studied affect as an additional communication channel for SAR robots: across three online studies with 112, 223 and 151 participants, LED-based affective expressions on an appearance-constrained field robot improved the situational awareness of participants acting as rescue workers in simulated SAR situations [@akgun2023]. Chitikena et al. discuss the ethical and design issues raised by robotic and cognitive technology in the response phase of SAR, with emphasis on snake robots for confined spaces [@chitikena2023].

**Victim side.** In the platforms reviewed here the trapped person is the object of detection rather than a party to communication. Victims are detected by vision, thermal sensing or multimodal fusion [@raghavan2026; @khattab2025], or the platform provides no victim-facing channel at all [@megalingam2022; @verano2022; @cafolla2026; @jia2025]. The one system that provisions a speaker and microphone for communication with victims reports that this capability is pending implementation in its prototype [@khattab2025]. A two-way channel through which a trapped person can see and hear the operator, and be seen and heard in turn, is therefore not demonstrated by any platform in Table 2.1 (C3).

## 2.5 Real-Time Media and Computing on Edge Hardware

WebRTC has been shown to meet strict latency targets in robot teleoperation. Shi et al. built a remote dental surgery robot on an optimised Amazon Kinesis Video Streams WebRTC framework with two video and two data channels and report end-to-end latency below 50 ms, 34.2% lower than their baseline configuration [@shi2025webrtc]. That system relies on a cloud signalling service; the robot described here runs both WebRTC endpoints and signalling on the local network with no internet dependence (Sections 3.2 and 3.9). For audio, the Opus codec is designed for interactive speech and audio and supports frame sizes from 2.5 ms to 60 ms [@valin2012], which lets a design trade packet rate against delay; Section 3.9.5 uses 60 ms packets instead of the usual 20 ms to cut per-packet CPU work on the Raspberry Pi. Comparing such systems requires a defined latency metric: Bachhuber and Steinbach present a glass-to-glass delay measurement system with 0.5 ms precision at a 2 kHz sampling rate [@bachhuber2016]; Section 5.2 reports the same glass-to-glass metric, measured with a simpler clock-photograph procedure.

Where the computing runs is itself a design choice. Raghavan et al. run detection onboard on a Jetson Nano but send alerts through the cloud [@raghavan2026], and Cafolla et al. use cloud-based connectivity for streaming and data flow [@cafolla2026]; both presume network infrastructure that a collapsed site may not provide. Running control, two-way media and supervision together on a single-board computer without the cloud is not characterised for a rescue robot in these works; Section 5.5 reports the resulting resource budget.

The gap that Table 2.1 makes visible is a combination rather than a single missing feature. Teleoperation is the mode used in real deployments [@daun2023] and is expected to remain a default mode [@chen2007], yet the platforms reviewed here invest in mechanical capability or perception accuracy. Within that work, no platform reports a stop time that is bounded independently of its higher-level software (C1) or supervised recovery of its software processes (C2); the trapped person appears as a detection target, and the one planned voice channel to victims is not yet implemented [@khattab2025] (C3); and low cost is asserted without a figure (C4). Section 3 describes a system designed to meet all four challenges on commodity hardware, and Section 5 reports which of these properties the current measurements support and which remain pending.
