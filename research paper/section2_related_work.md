# 2. Related Work

This section places the system within the search-and-rescue (SAR) robotics literature along the four challenges of Section 1.2: a bounded stop time (C1), freedom from single points of failure (C2), two-way victim interaction under constrained resources (C3) and affordability (C4). Sections 2.1 to 2.5 cover prior reviews, field deployments and platforms, fail-safe architectures, human-robot interaction (HRI) and real-time media; Section 2.6 states the gap and compares platforms in Table 2.1.

The search was not systematic and no PRISMA flow is claimed. In October 2026 OpenAlex returned 446 articles and conference papers from 2022 onward for "search and rescue robot teleoperation low-cost", 272 for "rescue robot victim communication two-way audio", 56 for "WebRTC robot teleoperation", 1 for "fail-safe watchdog dead-man teleoperated mobile robot" and 1,204 for "rescue robot Raspberry Pi"; these counts show how large the surrounding literature is, not how many records were screened. Candidates were selected from the relevance-ranked results by title and abstract and by backward citation from the reviews of Section 2.1. ## 2.1 Prior Reviews and Levels of Operator Involvement

Reviews of SAR robotics organise the field along different axes: a domain theory of robot-assisted search drawn from eight years of HRI experience [@murphy2004hri], the control perspective [@liu2013], multi-robot coordination and perception [@queralta2020], and readiness for deployment, where Delmerico et al. observe that field-deployable rescue technology has requirements for robustness, speed, versatility and ease of use that the state of the art may not meet [@delmerico2019]. Schneider and Wildermuth set out SAR requirements on each component of a robot [@schneider2016]. Recent reviews organise the field by system architecture, from 74 papers of 2016-2024 [@kareem2026]; by platform class, from more than 190 studies of unmanned ground vehicles (UGVs) of 2020-2025 [@sinha2026]; by perception deficiencies [@tong2024]; by ethical and design issues [@chitikena2023]; and, for mines, by design requirements, finding that two-way voice is a fundamental functionality of most mine rescue robots [@bakzadeh2026]. The open problems they name include multi-robot coordination, real-time decision-making, energy and standardised benchmarks [@kareem2026; @sinha2026]; neither the timing of the stop path nor the cost of victim interaction on the robot is the axis along which they organise the field.

Levels of robot autonomy range from teleoperation to fully autonomous systems [@beer2014], and SAR robots are in practice either tele-operated or semi-autonomous [@schneider2016]. This paper distinguishes Teleoperation (the operator drives the robot directly), Supervisory Control (the operator sets goals that the robot executes), Shared-Collaborative Control (operator and robot share decision authority) and Full Autonomy (no human in the control loop during the mission). The system presented here is a Teleoperation system, because rescue robots are typically teleoperated in actual disasters [@daun2023] and teleoperation is expected to remain an important default mode even for autonomous systems [@chen2007]. Higher levels, which would combine the complementary capabilities of operators and assistance functions [@daun2023], are treated in Section 8 as extensions subordinate to the stop guarantee of Section 3.5.

## 2.2 Field Deployments, Benchmarks and Recent Platforms

**Field deployments.** The response to the World Trade Center collapse in September 2001 is the first known use of robots for urban search and rescue [@murphy2004]; its post-hoc analysis, the first study of HRI during an unstaged rescue, called for perceptual and assistive interfaces and formal models of the robot's state [@casper2003]. At Fukushima Daiichi in 2011, tests in a comparable plant indicated that robust wireless communication would not be possible inside the reactor buildings, so the robot deployed there was fitted with a wired communication device [@nagatani2013]; organisation, operation scheme and systemisation were the major lessons [@kawatsuma2012]. After the 2016 Amatrice earthquake, ground and aerial robots collected data for 3D models of two damaged churches [@kruijff2016]. Because the Fukushima deployment had to be designed around the radio link failing, this paper treats link loss as an expected operating condition rather than a rare fault (C1, C2).

**Benchmarks.** Standard test methods for response robots have been refined through competitions [@jacoff2012], and the DARPA Subterranean Challenge (2018-2021) evaluated integrated systems in physical and simulated underground contests [@chung2023]. Section 5 does not use the standardised apparatus, so its results are not comparable with benchmark scores.

**Recent platforms.** The platforms in Table 2.1 concentrate their contribution on mobility, detection or communication range rather than on the control path. They range from a tracked robot with flippers and a manipulator scored at the World Robot Summit 2020 [@megalingam2022] to commodity designs: TXRob streams camera, microphone and gas data from a Raspberry Pi 4 over local Wi-Fi [@verano2022], and Pramono et al. combine ESP32 controllers, Bluetooth control and Internet-connected video [@pramono2024]. Others extend range with a LoRa network that needs no infrastructure [@manuel2022], add voice control and person tracking to existing robots through a cloud-connected controller [@cafolla2026], or change the mobility class, as in a caged ground-air UAV [@jia2025] and an active scope camera with 80 distributed sensors [@yamauchi2022]. Detection-oriented work locates survivors with a thermal infrared array [@kim2023], a YOLOv8 detector on a Jetson Nano [@raghavan2026], multimodal CNN-LSTM fusion validated in simulation [@khattab2025], or Bluetooth Low Energy wearables detected up to 15 m away at smartphone-level transmit power [@cantizani2022]. Affordability is claimed more often than quantified: four of these designs are described as low-cost, affordable or built from inexpensive parts [@verano2022; @pramono2024; @kim2023; @raghavan2026], yet none in Table 2.1 states a numeric build cost, so Section 5.9 reports this robot's figure as an estimate (C4).

## 2.3 Fail-Safe and Fault-Tolerant Architectures for Low-Cost Mobile Robots

**Bounded stop.** IEC 60204-1 defines stop categories for machinery, of which category 0 is stopping by immediate removal of power to the machine actuators, an uncontrolled stop [@iec60204]. The dead-man principle is not new; for a low-cost robot the questions are where the stop decision runs and whether its timing can be bounded, since a decision taken by a Linux process inherits its scheduling and crash behaviour. Pont presents patterns for time-triggered embedded systems, in which tasks are released by a periodic timer so that their timing can be predicted [@pont2001]. The firmware of Section 3.5 uses a co-operative, time-based scheduler in this style, with about 50 ms of loop jitter from two blocking sensor reads: a 10 ms safety task sets the pulse-width modulation (PWM) outputs to zero and pulls the driver-enable line low when no valid command arrives within the dead-man window. This behaves like a category-0 stop, removing drive at once and independently of Linux (C1), but it is not a safety-rated implementation in the sense of the standard. A run-time monitor for networked robots under delayed observations [@narasimhan2024] addresses late information; the dead-man covers commands that stop arriving, so the two are complementary.

**Process supervision.** Candea et al. argue that systems built to recover fast may be more productive than systems built never to fail, with multiple lines of defence because recovery can itself fail [@candea2004]. Schrick et al. use microservices and a hierarchical state machine to increase the interruptibility and fail-safety of a delivery robot [@schrick2025], Ramesh et al. estimate "robot health" at run time so that a supervisor can intervene [@ramesh2022], and a review of humanoid-robot safety finds cybersecurity and standardisation addressed less often than physical safety [@koczi2025]. None targets a single-board rescue robot. Section 3.7 applies these principles on a Raspberry Pi: a supervisor restarts the control and media processes independently, the firmware stop depends on neither, and the mission state exposes degradation to the operator (C2). No platform in Table 2.1 reports a hardware-level stop guarantee or supervised process recovery.

## 2.4 Human-Robot Interaction: Operator and Victim

**Operator side.** Situation awareness, modelled by Endsley through its role in dynamic decision making [@endsley1995], is the recurring operator-side concern. Chen et al. summarise the performance decrements that limited video bandwidth, time lags, low frame rates and two-dimensional views cause in teleoperation [@chen2007], and Luz et al., for planetary exploration, report that even small latencies can compromise robot safety and raise workload [@luz2023]. For mine rescue, interface design principles have been mapped from cognitive load, situational awareness and trust [@bakzadeh2025], and rescue personnel report a need for real-time situational data and customisable interfaces [@bakzadeh2026b]. Semantic indicators can signal when a scene needs the expert operator's attention [@ruan2025], and LED-based affective expressions improved participants' situational awareness in an online study [@akgun2023]. The dashboard of Section 3.6 serves this goal with telemetry staleness timeouts and a mission state.

**Victim side.** A SAR robot should be able to provide audio-visual communication between the operator and trapped persons that allows basic diagnosis by an emergency responder [@schneider2016], and on average four to ten hours pass between discovery and extrication, during which the robot must keep the victim calm [@srinivasan2012]. Bethel and Murphy applied non-verbal affective expression to victim assessment within about three metres of a victim [@bethel2007]. Survivor Buddy, a four-degree-of-freedom head with a touch-screen face, was built to study robots as social media between trapped survivors and the outside world [@murphy2010]; its second version was 50% less expensive and 78% lighter [@murphy2011]. It was evaluated in an informal demonstration and in simulated scenarios [@murphy2010; @srinivasan2012]. Two-way voice is also a fundamental functionality of most mine rescue robots, such as the ANDROS Wolverine V2 [@bakzadeh2026], which are specialised platforms outside the low-cost scope of Table 2.1.

Among recent platforms the trapped person is mainly an object of detection [@raghavan2026; @khattab2025; @kim2023; @cantizani2022]. TXRob's microphone relays sound to the operator only [@verano2022]; and the one platform in Table 2.1 that provisions a speaker and microphone for victims has not yet implemented it [@khattab2025]. Two-way victim communication is therefore an established requirement and a feature of fielded mine-rescue robots; what no recent low-cost platform in Table 2.1 demonstrates is two-way audio and video with the victim on single-board hardware, or its CPU, power and bandwidth cost (C3).

## 2.5 Real-Time Media and Computing on Edge Hardware

WebRTC has been used for teleoperation with strict latency targets: a remote dental surgery robot on Amazon Kinesis Video Streams WebRTC reports end-to-end latency below 50 ms, 34.2% lower than its baseline [@shi2025webrtc], and a multi-operator platform for a mobile manipulator, validated over the Internet, uses dedicated web and signalling servers and Google's public STUN servers [@tiberkak2023]; Sections 3.8 and 3.9.4 address the same multi-operator problem with arbitration and floor control. Both depend on servers outside the robot's network, and TXRob streams one-way video over local Wi-Fi without WebRTC [@verano2022]; the robot described here runs both WebRTC endpoints and signalling on the local network (Sections 3.2, 3.9). Opus supports frame sizes from 2.5 ms to 60 ms [@valin2012]; Section 3.9.5 uses 60 ms packets instead of the 20 ms frames the specification describes as a good choice for most applications, to cut per-packet CPU work. Bachhuber and Steinbach present a glass-to-glass delay measurement system with 0.5 ms precision [@bachhuber2016]; Section 5.2 defines the same metric, to be measured with a coarser clock-photograph procedure, and that measurement is pending.

The cloud-to-edge-to-IoT continuum has been proposed to meet the low-delay requirements of SAR, though building it remains an open challenge [@militano2023]. Recent platforms send alerts or streams through the cloud [@raghavan2026; @cafolla2026], presuming infrastructure that a collapsed site may not provide, the concern behind infrastructure-free LoRa [@manuel2022] and the wired link at Fukushima [@nagatani2013]. Running control, two-way media and supervision together on a single-board computer without the cloud is not characterised for a rescue robot in these works; Section 5.5 reports that budget.

The gap that Table 2.1 makes visible is a combination rather than a single missing feature, and none of its parts is claimed as a first. Teleoperation is the mode used in real deployments [@daun2023], and the link to the operator fails in the field [@nagatani2013]. Stop categories are standardised [@iec60204] and recovery by restart is an established dependability strategy [@candea2004], yet, in the text read for Table 2.1 (see its caption), no recent platform reports a stop time bounded independently of its higher-level software (C1) or supervised recovery of its processes (C2). Two-way communication with the trapped person is a stated requirement [@schneider2016], a feature of fielded mine-rescue robots [@bakzadeh2026] and the subject of an HRI literature [@bethel2007; @murphy2010; @srinivasan2012], yet among recent low-cost platforms the victim is mainly a detection target, the one planned voice channel is not implemented [@khattab2025], and none reports two-way audio and video with the victim or its resource cost (C3). Low cost is asserted without a figure (C4). Section 3 brings these established mechanisms together on commodity hardware, and Section 5 reports which properties the current measurements support and which remain pending.
