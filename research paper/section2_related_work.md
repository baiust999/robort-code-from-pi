# 2. Related Work

This section places the system within the search-and-rescue (SAR) robotics literature along the four challenges of Section 1.2: a bounded stop time (C1), freedom from single points of failure (C2), two-way victim interaction under constrained resources (C3) and affordability (C4). Section 2.1 positions the work against prior reviews and the autonomy/human-robot interaction (HRI) taxonomy used throughout the paper. Sections 2.2 to 2.5 review teleoperated rescue platforms, fail-safe architectures, operator- and victim-side interaction, and real-time media on edge hardware. Section 2.6 states the gap and compares representative platforms in Table 2.1. Statements about individual papers are taken from those papers; corpus-level figures for 2025-2026 come from the authors' companion systematic review [@avi2026review], which screened 130 candidate records to 39 included papers under PRISMA 2020.

## 2.1 Prior Reviews and the Autonomy/HRI Taxonomy

Reviews of SAR robotics differ mainly in the axis along which they organise their corpus. Liu and Nejat surveyed robotic urban search and rescue from the control perspective [@liu2013]. Kareem et al. analysed 74 papers published between 2016 and 2024, classified platforms as aerial, ground or marine, and proposed a five-layer architecture (HRI, Perception, Navigation, Control and Robot layers) [@kareem2026]; HRI is one layer of the five, discussed across teleoperation, supervisory control and collaborative control, without a separate category for full autonomy [@avi2026review]. Sinha et al. synthesised more than 190 studies from 2020 to 2025 restricted to unmanned ground vehicles (UGVs) for disaster reconnaissance, identifying energy limitations, robustness in harsh conditions and the lack of standardised benchmarks as open problems [@sinha2026]. Bakzadeh et al. reviewed three decades of coal-mine rescue robots against physical design and functionality requirements [@bakzadeh2026], and Tong et al. reviewed robotised SAR with an emphasis on deficiencies in perception systems [@tong2024]. None of these reviews resolves its corpus by how much autonomy the human-robot interaction affords.

The companion review [@avi2026review] adopts that axis and assigns every included system to one of four modes: Teleoperation, in which the operator drives the robot directly; Supervisory Control, in which the operator sets goals and the robot executes them; Shared-Collaborative Control, in which operator and robot share decision authority; and Fully Autonomous, in which no human is in the mission-time control loop. In the 2025-2026 corpus, Fully Autonomous systems account for 27 of 39 papers (69.2%), Teleoperation for 5 (12.8%), Supervisory Control for 4 (10.3%) and Shared-Collaborative Control for 3 (7.7%). The modes differ sharply in how they are validated: three of five Teleoperation papers (60%) and three of four Supervisory Control papers (75%) report a real-world field test, against 2 of 27 Fully Autonomous papers (7.4%), 16 of which (59.3%) are validated in simulation only [@avi2026review].

The system presented here is a Teleoperation system in this taxonomy. The choice is deliberate rather than a limitation of ambition: in actual disaster deployments rescue robots are typically teleoperated [@daun2023], and the mode that is studied least in the current literature is also the mode most often validated outside simulation. Autonomy above this tier is treated in Section 8 as an extension that must remain subordinate to the stop guarantee of Section 3.5.

## 2.2 Teleoperated Rescue Platforms and Their Cost

The case for a human in the control loop rests on field evidence. Casper and Murphy's post-hoc analysis of robot use at the World Trade Center, the first study of HRI during an unstaged rescue, produced 17 findings and 11 recommendations, including calls for more research into perceptual and assistive interfaces and for formal models of the robot's state [@casper2003]. Daun and von Stryk, drawing on past deployments, their own deployment experience and workshops with first responders, report that rescue robots are typically teleoperated in actual disasters and identify a need for approaches that combine the complementary capabilities of human operators and robotic assistance functions [@daun2023].

The five teleoperated systems in the 2025-2026 corpus share one pattern: locomotion remains human-driven, and the contribution lies elsewhere [@avi2026review]. Three contribute mechanical or platform capability. Cafolla et al. built a universal add-on controller that connects to any robot accepting text commands and adds voice-guided control, 3D reconstruction and person tracking, tested on a drone and a quadruped in wind of 23 km/h and noise up to 100 dB [@cafolla2026]. Jia et al. designed a terrestrial-aerial UAV whose separation-cage structure removes the sensor occlusion of earlier caged drones, with a 1.2 kg prototype reaching 40 min endurance at full 1 kg payload [@jia2025]. Liu et al. designed a wheeled-hexapod rescue device with a 1,200 kg payload and a hierarchical control layer that stabilises body attitude on uneven terrain [@liu2026hexapod; @avi2026review]. The other two contribute perception. Raghavan et al. fine-tuned a YOLOv8 model to detect victims under rubble (93.1% mean average precision) and deployed it on a Jetson Nano carried by a mobile robot with cloud-based alerting [@raghavan2026]. Khattab et al. fused visual, thermal and audio data in a CNN-LSTM survivor detector validated by MATLAB simulation on synthetic USGS-PAGER data, on a smartphone-controlled robot [@khattab2025]. Navigation autonomy is explicitly deferred to future work in three of the five [@khattab2025; @jia2025; @liu2026hexapod]. None of the five reports a bounded stop time or process supervision as a contribution (Table 2.1).

The same pattern appears in older platforms. Paripreksya 2.0 is a teleoperated tracked robot with four assistive flippers and a 4-DoF manipulator, built on ROS and evaluated through scores at the World Robot Summit 2020, where it received the best-in-class dexterity award [@megalingam2022]. TXRob is a low-cost tracked teleoperated explorer with a camera, gas sensors and a real-time data display, tested on uneven surfaces in a simulated disaster environment [@verano2022]. Outside general SAR, mine and tunnel rescue robotics has remained predominantly teleoperation-oriented [@bakzadeh2026], whereas none of the six underground scenarios in the 2025-2026 corpus uses Teleoperation as its primary mode [@avi2026review].

Affordability is claimed more often than it is quantified. TXRob is described as low-cost [@verano2022], Raghavan et al. describe the Jetson Nano as a low-cost processor [@raghavan2026], and Sinha et al. identify microcontroller-based control, LoRa communication and DC motors as recurring building blocks of current reconnaissance UGVs [@sinha2026]; yet none of the platforms in Table 2.1 states a numeric build cost. Section 5.9 therefore compares cost qualitatively and reports this robot's own figure as an estimate (C4).

## 2.3 Fail-Safe and Fault-Tolerant Architectures for Low-Cost Mobile Robots

A bounded stop time requires that the stop decision does not depend on a non-real-time operating system. Pont presents design patterns for time-triggered embedded systems, in which tasks are released by a periodic timer rather than by asynchronous events, so that their timing can be predicted [@pont2001]. The firmware of Section 3.5 follows this pattern: a 10 ms safety task on the microcontroller removes motor drive when no valid command has arrived within the dead-man window, independently of the Linux processes above it (C1).

Above the firmware, the risk is a single software process whose failure takes down both control and media. Schrick et al. propose a robot control architecture built from microservices and a hierarchical finite-state machine that reduces centralised logic and so increases concurrency, interruptibility and fail-safety, and demonstrate it on a delivery robot operated in public space [@schrick2025]. Koczi and Sarosi, in a scoping review of 121 studies on safety engineering for humanoid robots, find that most work concentrates on physical safety, including fault-tolerant control, while software robustness, secure real-time communication and standardisation receive less attention [@koczi2025]. Both works concern platforms and budgets well above a single-board rescue robot. The three-process design of Section 3.7, in which a supervisor restarts the control and media processes independently, applies the same isolation principle on a Raspberry Pi (C2). Among the rescue platforms of Table 2.1, none reports a hardware-level stop guarantee or supervised process recovery.

## 2.4 Human-Robot Interaction: Operator and Victim

**Operator side.** Chen et al. reviewed more than 150 papers on human performance in teleoperation and summarise the performance decrements caused by limited video bandwidth, time lags, low frame rates, lack of proprioception and two-dimensional views; they also argue that teleoperation will remain an important default mode even for autonomous and semi-autonomous systems [@chen2007]. For mine rescue, Bakzadeh et al. map cognitive load, situational awareness, trust and attentional control to interface design principles and recommend layered feedback, multimodal communication and adaptive interfaces [@bakzadeh2025]. Ruan et al. propose semantic indicators, including signs of human activity, that summarise how information-rich a scene is and so when it requires the expert operator's attention, tested on a Jackal robot in a mock-up disaster environment [@ruan2025]. These works treat the operator as the human the interface serves; the dashboard of this system (Section 3.6) follows the same goal through telemetry with explicit staleness timeouts and a mission state (Section 3.7.3).

**Robot to rescue team.** Akgun et al. studied affect as an additional communication channel for SAR robots: across three online studies with 112, 223 and 151 participants, LED-based affective expressions on an appearance-constrained field robot improved the situational awareness of participants acting as rescue workers in simulated SAR situations [@akgun2023]. Chitikena et al. discuss the ethical and design issues raised by robotic and cognitive technology in the response phase of SAR, with emphasis on snake robots for confined spaces [@chitikena2023].

**Victim side.** In this literature the trapped person is the object of detection rather than a party to communication. The five 2025-2026 teleoperated systems detect victims by vision, thermal sensing or multimodal fusion [@raghavan2026; @khattab2025], or provide no victim-facing channel at all [@cafolla2026; @jia2025; @liu2026hexapod]. The one system that provisions a speaker and microphone for communication with victims reports that this capability is pending implementation in its prototype [@khattab2025]. A two-way channel through which a trapped person can see and hear the operator, and be seen and heard in turn, is therefore not demonstrated by any system in Table 2.1 (C3). ## 2.5 Real-Time Media and Computing on Edge Hardware

WebRTC has been shown to meet strict latency targets in robot teleoperation. Shi et al. built a remote dental surgery robot on an optimised Amazon Kinesis Video Streams WebRTC framework with two video and two data channels and report end-to-end latency below 50 ms, 34.2% lower than their baseline configuration [@shi2025webrtc]. That system relies on a cloud signalling service; the robot described here runs both WebRTC endpoints and signalling on the local network with no internet dependence (Sections 3.2 and 3.9). For audio, the Opus codec is designed for interactive speech and audio and supports frame sizes from 2.5 ms to 60 ms [@valin2012], which lets a design trade packet rate against delay; Section 3.9.5 uses 60 ms packets instead of the usual 20 ms to cut per-packet CPU work on the Raspberry Pi. Comparing such systems requires a defined latency metric: Bachhuber and Steinbach present a glass-to-glass delay measurement system with 0.5 ms precision at a 2 kHz sampling rate [@bachhuber2016]; Section 5.2 reports the same glass-to-glass metric, measured with a simpler clock-photograph procedure.

Where the computing runs is itself a design choice. In the 2025-2026 corpus, PC or offline computation is the largest category (15 papers, 38.5%), followed by onboard/edge deployment (13, 33.3%), runtime-hybrid architectures (10, 25.6%) and cloud as the primary architecture (1, 2.6%) [@avi2026review]. Two of the teleoperated systems use cloud connectivity for streaming or alerts [@cafolla2026; @raghavan2026], which presumes network infrastructure that a collapsed site may not provide. Running control, two-way media and supervision together on a single-board computer without the cloud has not been characterised for a rescue robot in these works; Section 5.5 reports the resulting resource budget.

The gap that Table 2.1 makes visible is a combination rather than a single missing feature. Teleoperation is the mode used in real deployments [@daun2023] and the mode most often field-validated [@avi2026review], yet it is the second-smallest mode in current research, and the teleoperated systems that exist invest in mechanical capability or perception accuracy [@avi2026review]. Within that work, no platform reports a stop time that is bounded independently of its higher-level software (C1) or supervised recovery of its software processes (C2); the trapped person appears as a detection target, and the one planned voice channel to victims is not yet implemented [@khattab2025] (C3); and low cost is asserted without a figure (C4). Section 3 describes a system designed to meet all four challenges on commodity hardware, and Section 5 reports which of these properties the current measurements support and which remain pending.
