# 4. Experimental Setup

This section defines the test environment, the experiment matrix, the measurement and validity rules, and the baselines that apply across all twelve experiments. Each subsection of Section 5 opens with its own procedure paragraph; shared conditions stated here are not repeated there. The hardware, network and software are specified in Sections 3.2-3.4 and are referenced, not restated. Because several experiments could not be run as planned, Table 4.1 gives the planned design and the design actually achieved side by side, and Section 4.4 lists every deviation; Section 5 reports only what was achieved. For every experiment that still has to be run, Table 4.2 names the claim it tests and the pass criterion, both fixed before any data is collected, so that a result cannot be reinterpreted after the fact.

## 4.1 Test Conditions

All experiments run on the robot as built (Table 2, Figure 3) with a single operator laptop connected to the same Wi-Fi cell, except where Table 4.1 states that a probe ran on the Pi itself. The network is the 802.11n cell described in Section 3.2: 2.4 GHz, 20 MHz width, no internet uplink, with the access point nominally on channel 10. The channel was read at the start of the E6 sessions and was not constant across the campaign (channel 3 for two stations on 2026-10-22, channel 10 thereafter; Section 5.3), so the channel is recorded per station rather than assumed. The robot runs Raspberry Pi OS 12 (Bookworm, kernel 6.12, arm64) with Python 3.11, and the operator uses Chromium on the same laptop for the dashboard. Hardware emulation is disabled for every run, so every command reaches the physical Arduino and motors.

The control-latency probe (E1) is the one exception to the laptop-to-robot path: it runs on the Pi and connects to P1 over the loopback interface, so its figures exclude the Wi-Fi link and the operator laptop (Section 5.1). Link-dependent behaviour is covered separately by E6 and by the SIMULATED heartbeat-loss model of Section 5.1b, which is not a measurement.

Three test sites are used:

- Indoor laboratory (approximately 6 x 8 m): control-latency, fault-injection, resource-utilisation, victim-communication (E17) and detector test-set (E18) experiments; for E17 and E18 the room illumination and the background noise level are set and recorded per condition, since they are the manipulated variables there. The operator and robot are within 3 m of the router, so the link is not a variable.
- Indoor corridor and adjoining rooms: field-trial course (Section 5.7).
- Outdoor open area: Wi-Fi range sweep (Section 5.3) and GPS static precision (Section 5.6), where line-of-sight distances up to link failure are needed.

Each result is tied to the test date and to the log or CSV it came from. Dates, labels and session boundaries are part of the evidence and are reported in Section 5, because several quantities (E1 and E6 in particular) differed between sessions.

**Ethics and human-subject protocol.** Two experiments involve people other than the experimenter: the field trial (E13, Section 5.7), in which the operator is one of the authors and a second person role-plays the victim and talks with the operator through the robot's display, camera, microphone and speaker; and the victim-communication study (E17, Section 5.8), in which fifteen participants each role-play the victim under three communication modes and two victim states. One ethics application covers both, because the exposure is the same kind in each: a short, voluntary, non-invasive communication task in a controlled indoor room. Participants are briefed on the task, the data collected and their right to withdraw at any time before the session begins; all give informed consent, participation is voluntary, no deception is involved, and responses are recorded anonymously with a participant number only. No personally identifiable data is collected or stored. In E17 the participant may be lying down and partly covered by a blanket in one victim state, is free to stop at any moment, and is never restrained or enclosed; nothing is recorded on the robot, and no image or recording of a participant is stored on the Pi (Section 3.11). The tasks are short teleoperation and communication exercises in a controlled indoor environment, which pose minimal risk. The detector test set (E18, Section 5.9) shows only the authors, who consent to their own frames being captured and labelled; those frames are held on the operator's computer and are not stored on the robot, and none is published. [PENDING: the ethics-committee decision or institutional waiver, with its reference number and date, must be stated here before submission; the paper does not assert an exemption on its own authority.] No human-subject data has been collected to date (Sections 5.7 and 5.8).

**Pass/fail thresholds.** The design in Section 3 defines four timing guarantees that the experiments must validate:

1. The dead-man stop bound of approximately 2060 ms (Section 3.5.2), tested by E4 and, for the firmware-side interval, by E1.
2. The telemetry timeout of 3000 ms that triggers mission state STOP at the operator (Section 3.7.3, Table 10). This is a dashboard design constant. No experiment measured it, so it is a specification target only and Section 5 reports no result for it.
3. The recovery-time model of Section 3.7.2 (14.6-19.6 s for a P1 crash, including 10 s cooldown), tested by E7.
4. The lockout threshold of 5 key failures within 300 s (Section 3.8). It is checked only by software tests (`pi/tests/test_controller_access.py`, 25 of 25 passed on 2026-10-23, part of the suite of Section 5.1a), not by a trial on the robot; the evidence class is SOFTWARE-TEST.

In addition, the GPS track filter of 10 m (Section 3.10) sets the boundary below which static drift produces no spurious track points, tested by E9.

**Criteria fixed before data collection.** Each experiment that has not yet been run, or whose data must be repeated, is listed in Table 4.2 with the claim it tests, the pass criterion and what a failure would mean for the paper. The criteria were written down before any of these data were collected. Where a limit is a design constant of Section 3 or a CALCULATED bound, the table says so; where it is a limit chosen by the authors, it is marked "adopted" and is not presented as a property of the robot or as a requirement from a standard. A result is reported against its criterion whether it passes or fails, and a criterion is not changed after its data have been seen.

**Quantisation and resolution.** The wslatency probe (E1) waits for the telemetry path to confirm a command. Because the Arduino emits telemetry every 500 ms and P1 rebroadcasts on a 200 ms timer (Section 3.6.2), the confirmation can arrive up to 700 ms after the event, making E1 figures upper bounds rather than true latencies. No direct measurement of control latency over Wi-Fi is part of this evaluation, so E1 is reported only as an upper bound. The deadman_panic timestamp in the P1 log has millisecond resolution and is not subject to telemetry quantisation. E1 uses a 0-200 ms random jitter on the send phase (Section 3.6.2) to decorrelate trials from the P1 rebroadcast cycle; without this jitter a phase-locked sender produces a biased, deceptively tight distribution, which the phase-locked session documents. The jitter cannot decorrelate from the 500 ms firmware cycle, which is a known limitation.

**Communication-range protocol and criterion (E6).** The sweep measures the Pi-to-router radio link, not the operator-to-robot path end to end. At each station `netsweep.py` sends a burst of 100 ICMP echo requests at 0.2 s intervals to the router and logs loss, mean and maximum round-trip time, the signal level at the start and end of the burst, the negotiated transmit bitrate and the channel; the operator then enters the distance, the obstacle description and two judgements (video: good, choppy or lost; control: yes, degraded or no). Three limits follow from this design and are carried into the interpretation in Section 5.3. First, ICMP loss to the router is not the loss rate of the control heartbeat or of the WebRTC media, so it is a proxy for link quality and not a measure of mission-level behaviour. Second, one burst per station gives no estimate of burst-to-burst variation, so a distance effect cannot be separated from a time effect or sampling noise without repeated bursts at a fixed station. Third, distances and the video and control judgements are operator-entered and unverified. The usable range is defined a priori as the largest distance at which both operator judgements are favourable (video good, control yes) and ICMP loss is below 5%. The 5% limit is a criterion adopted by this paper, not a measured tolerance of the robot. Where the two conditions disagree, Section 5.3 reports both and does not select one after the fact. Extending the sweep beyond 250 m and adding obstructed links is left to future work (Section 7.1.1).

**Validity rule.** A trial is valid when the system is fully started (all three processes healthy, serial link active, no prior fault injected and not cleared), the network link is stable at the test distance, and the tool reports `ok = true`. A trial where the WebSocket connection fails to open, or where an unrelated fault occurs during measurement, is discarded and logged with a reason in the `detail` or `notes` column. Discarded trials are reported in Section 5 alongside the valid count, not silently dropped. For E6 the "stable link" condition cannot be a validity filter, since link degradation is the quantity measured; every logged station is therefore retained, including the repeat stations R1 to R6, and the sessions are reported separately.

**Data integrity and provenance.** Rows written by the automated tools (E1, E6, E7, E8) are machine-generated. Rows entered by hand through `record.py` (the manual fault watch of E7, and the pending E4, E5 and E13 sheets) carry the time of data entry, not of measurement, and are accepted only where the robot's independent logs (`p1_events.log`, `telemetry.log`) agree with them; the cross-check is summarised in Table 5.0. A result that the logs contradict or cannot support is not used to support any claim. Each reported number carries an evidence class (MEASURED, CALCULATED, SIMULATED, [PENDING MEASUREMENT] and the others defined at the head of Section 5), so a SIMULATED or SOFTWARE-TEST figure cannot be read as a physical measurement.

**Statistical treatment.** Interval estimates, the handling of autocorrelated 1 Hz series, and the decision not to report hypothesis tests are stated once, at the head of Section 5, and are reproduced by `tools/eval/section5_stats.py`. With the small and unequal sample sizes of Table 4.1, differences between conditions are described, not tested.

E17 is the one exception, and it is stated here before its data exist. It is a designed within-subjects experiment with equal cells and a planned comparison, so a description without a test would waste the design. The analysis fixed in advance is: for each victim state, Cochran's Q across the three communication modes on the per-participant binary outcome "all three items transferred correctly"; if and only if Q is significant at the 0.05 level, exact McNemar tests on the three mode pairs with Holm correction; and, as the reported effect measure, the difference in proportions between modes with a 95% confidence interval, which is given whether or not any test is significant. No other experiment in Table 4.1 is tested, and this plan is not changed after the data are seen.

## 4.3 Baselines

Three kinds of baseline frame the results.

**Internal comparisons.** Two comparisons isolate the effect of a design choice on this robot:

1. Media isolation (E16). The question is whether the media path can delay or block the control path, which is the reason control and media run in separate processes. It is tested on the robot as built: the E1 motor probe is run 30 times in each of three conditions, `media-off` (no operator media session), `media-on` (one operator session with video and two-way audio) and `p2-frozen` (P2 suspended with SIGSTOP in 15 s windows, each resumed with SIGCONT before the 35 s health-check path of Section 3.7.2 can restart it). The claim and pass criterion are in Table 4.2. Not run [PENDING MEASUREMENT]. The test shows whether the split isolates control from media on this robot; it does not show how a single merged process would behave, since none was built.
2. Alternative Opus bitrate (16 kbps vs the default 32 kbps): trades audio quality against CPU load, reported in Section 5.5. Its run is confounded by a P2 restart (Section 5.5).
3. Communication mode (E17). The three modes of Section 3.11 are compared within participants against each other, with two-way speech alone as the baseline the paper already claims, under two victim states. The comparison is internal to this robot: it shows which of its own aids carries information when speech fails, not that this robot communicates better than another platform.
4. Detection condition (E18). The strata of the labelled frame set are compared against each other at the shipped threshold. No second detector, threshold or camera is run, so the experiment bounds this configuration and makes no comparative claim about EfficientDet-Lite0 as a model.

The dead-man and the watchdog are evaluated by their measured behaviour with the feature present (Table 5.2b, E4 and E7); the paper claims no measured benefit over a system without them. Until E16 is run, it claims no measured isolation of the control path from the media path.

**Specification targets.** The timing constants of Section 3 serve as pass/fail lines: the 2000 ms dead-man window, the 3000 ms telemetry timeout, and the recovery-time model bounds. These are design specifications, not measured values; the experiments test whether the implementation meets them.

**Literature baselines.** The platforms of the Section 2.6 comparison (Table 2.1) are compared by their published descriptions and are not re-run on this hardware. The only quantitative comparison is cost (Table 5.16, [@verano2022], [@megalingam2022], [@casper2003], [@daun2023], [@liu2013]), and none of those papers reports a numeric build cost. The comparison in Sections 5.10 and 6.1 is therefore qualitative; it is not a controlled head-to-head experiment, and no cost ratio is computed.

## 4.4 Deviations from the Planned Design and Threats to Validity

The deviations below are collected here so that a reader of Section 5 can see at once where the achieved design departs from Table 4.1.

- **Substitute evidence for experiments not run.** Each substitute below is a weaker kind of evidence and is not equivalent to the planned experiment; Section 5 labels it with its own evidence class.
  - E4 (physical dead-man stop): replaced by the firmware-side interval timed on P1's clock (Table 5.2b, maximum 2042 ms in 30 of 30 trials), two fault observations (F7 and the unplanned heartbeat gap, Tables 5.7a and 5.7c) and the native test of the compiled firmware (Table 5.3d). These show when drive is removed, not when the wheels stop.
  - E5 (streaming): no substitute for delay or quality. E8 gives only the processor cost of one operator media session (Tables 5.8 and 5.8b).
  - E13 (field trial): no substitute. The fault tests of E7 and the software tests of Section 5.1a say nothing about rescue performance.
  - E17 (victim communication): no substitute. The unit tests and headless-browser checks of the question path (Section 3.11) show that a tapped answer reaches the operator, not that a person in a victim state can use it.
  - E18 (person detection): no substitute. The functional check of the CPU and GPU delegates (Section 3.11) used two photographs and is not an accuracy measurement.
  - Ethics: no human-subject data exist, so no approval reference is needed yet; it is required before E13 is run.
- **Experiments not run:** E4 (video-based), E5, E13, E16 (media isolation), E17 (victim communication) and E18 (person detection). They appear in Section 5 as [PENDING MEASUREMENT] with a protocol, the pass criterion of Table 4.2 and an empty table. Consequently no human-subject data exist, and the end-to-end rescue claim of the design is untested.
- **Order of the remaining work.** E18 comes first: it needs no ethics approval, no purchase and no participants, since the only people in frame are the authors, and it is the cheapest result of the six. E16 follows, since it needs no extra equipment and can be run in any session. E5 is next, as the only test of the media half of contribution C3. E4 needs a 240 fps camera and, with a tape laid along the path, also yields the distance covered after drive is removed. E13 and E17 are last and share one ethics application; E13 also depends on the media path that E5 characterises, and E17 cannot be run at all until a touch input is fitted to the robot display, because no answer has yet been given on the robot itself (Section 3.11). [AUTHOR ACTION: the ethics application and the touch input are the critical path for E13 and E17; neither can start without them.]
- **Reduced or changed designs:** E6 is a single-burst, line-of-sight, no-obstacle sweep that never reached a failure point; E8 has six conditions of unequal length, one of which (`driving+media`) is two overlapping logger runs and one of which (`driving+media-16kbps`) is confounded by a P2 restart; E9 uses an archived static window rather than a fresh 30-minute run.
- **Narrow fault coverage:** E7 injected only SIGKILL crashes automatically (no hangs, camera loss, microphone loss or Robot Screen exit). The manual faults F6 (Arduino unplug) and F7 (Wi-Fi drop) are single injections whose injection instants were not logged, and F9 (GPS loss) is not a valid measurement.
- **Configuration and cost records:** the 16 kbit/s Opus setting of the E8 ablation was applied by an uncommitted change and is not in version control. The replacement Pi supply is not identified, and the E15 bill of materials, now taken from the authors' itemised receipts (in BDT, converted at an indicative exchange rate), omits a few parts (the USB webcam, the speaker and the Pi power bank).
- **Features added after the measurements:** the victim-finding aids of Section 3.11 (person detection in the operator's browser, the repeating voice message, and victim marks), the session recording and the tap-to-answer questions were added after the measured experiments of Table 4.1 were run. E17 and E18 now cover the two of them that carry a claim, the victim channel and the detector, and both are [PENDING MEASUREMENT]; victim marks, session recording and the resource cost of the aids remain uncovered by any experiment. The E8 processor figures were recorded without them, and one further E8 condition with the voice loop running is planned to close that gap (Section 5.5). By design, detection and recording run on the operator's computer, the voice message is decoded once on upload and then fed to the existing Robot Screen audio track of Section 3.9.4, and questions add only short data-channel messages on P2, but none of these design properties has been measured.
- **Software-only checks:** the dead-man boundary and arming behaviour, the command validator and the key lockout were checked by unit tests and by a native build of the firmware sources (Section 5.1a), and the heartbeat-loss analysis (Section 5.1b) is SIMULATED. None is a measurement on the assembled robot.
- **Uncontrolled environment (E6):** the two range sessions differ by about 17 dB in signal level at the same distances, the access point changed channel between them, the router position and neighbouring networks were not recorded, and the 2.4 GHz band is shared. Range and loss figures are properties of this access point and site, not of the robot.
- **Single robot, single operator, single site per experiment:** results do not estimate between-unit or between-operator variation, and no experiment was repeated on a second unit.
- **Resolution limits:** GPS positions in the telemetry log carry four decimal places (about 11 x 10 m), so the E9 statistics are resolution-limited; E1 figures are telemetry-quantised upper bounds.
- **Bench confound:** the E8 `driving` conditions state that the wheels were clear of the ground on the operator's word only, which the logs cannot confirm.
